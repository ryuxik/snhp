# RESULTS v4 — Molt Season under PREREG AMENDMENT 3

**The equal-speed money claim is dead, permanently, by the rule registered before
the run.** It missed in v3 by $693. Given a promotion that is a real level change
— 12% raise, portable, rivalrous slots — it misses by **$3,506**.

| | equal-speed joint gain | threshold |
|---|---|---|
| v3 — 2% promotion, no scarcity | +$5,397 | $6,090 |
| **v4 — 12% raise, portable, 12% of crew promotable** | **+$2,584** | $6,090 |
| v4, held-out seed 101 | **+$1,739** | $6,090 |

Below the bar entirely on the held-out seed. K19's registered consequence binds:
**retired across two independently specified promotion models.**

Making the product's best currency bigger and better made it *worse*. That is the
opposite of what I predicted and the opposite of what the objection expected.

## The finding that matters: the archetype wins the promotion

Verifiable regime, main seeds. This is the table to read:

| arm | **crab CASH** | crab utility | promoted | the Works | joint | departures |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| just sign it | 4,913 | 13,636 | 0.0% | −37,749 | −24,113 | 29.4% |
| **six weeks of email** (Anchoring Bias) | **11,499** | 17,451 | **11.2%** | −52,823 | −35,372 | 35.8% |
| **one sitting** (the engine) | **8,104** | 18,457 | **2.3%** | −24,494 | −6,038 | 14.0% |

**The human negotiator gets the promotion five times as often, and $3,395 more
cash.** The engine wins only on a utility score computed with exchange rates I
chose.

Once a promotion costs the employer a real 12% raise out of the comp budget and a
scarce slot out of the band budget, the engine stops asking for it — 2.3% versus
11.2% — and buys retention with PTO and flexible hours instead, which are cheap.
The archetype keeps asking for the title, and sometimes gets it, and the title
carries a raise.

Selection-free, on the 1,188 crab-seasons retained under both protocols:
**cash $18,252 → $10,717, a $7,535 cut**, with the promotion rate falling
16% → 3%. Held-out seed: −$8,591, 18% → 3%.

## The sweep that shows the mechanism

As a promotion becomes more valuable, the engine uses it less and pays you in
time off instead:

| promotion raise | engine's promotion rate | equal-speed gain | crab CASH |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6% | 3.5% | +3,101 | −2,470 |
| 12% | 1.9% | +1,974 | −2,814 |
| 20% | **0.4%** | +1,813 | **−4,447** |

Monotone in all three columns. **The better a promotion actually is, the less an
optimiser will get you one.**

## Kill verdicts

| kill | verdict |
|---|---|
| **K19** the equal-speed claim, second specification | **FIRES** — +$2,584 and +$1,739, against $6,090. Retired permanently |
| **K20** does an optimiser allocate scarcity better | **FIRES** — engine targeting +0.049 vs archetype +0.337. Worse, not merely no better. *Instrument caveat below* |

### K20 fired, and the instrument is weak — say so

Registered test: correlation between granting a slot and the retention value it
buys. Engine **+0.049**, best archetype **+0.337**, on both seeds.

**But the engine grants promotions at 2.3% against the archetype's 11.2%**, and a
point-biserial correlation with a positive class that rare is mechanically
attenuated. So this number is partly measuring *how often you promote* rather
than *how well you aim*. The honest reading is the weaker one: **the engine does
not use the scarce resource, so it cannot be said to allocate it well.** Whether
it would aim better at equal grant rates is untested, and the clean version
conditions on grant rate.

K20's verdict stands as registered. Its interpretation does not extend past what
the instrument can carry — the same standard applied to K17 in v3, where I threw
a result out for being dead rather than let it read as a null.

### K21: promoted crabs do not leave — but nobody randomised this

Zero of 44 promoted crabs departed, against 14.3% of the rest. Portability
(+5pp on the outside premium) did not outweigh the 12% raise and the career
value.

**This is selection and I will not report it as anything else.** The employer
promotes the crabs it most wants to keep and that are most likely to stay. The
causal question — same crab, promoted versus not — needs a forced-promotion
counterfactual arm that does not exist. Given how badly the K17 instrument failed
in v3, building it before claiming anything is the only defensible order.

### K22: the cash cut survives, and grows

I predicted the cash effect would shrink once a promotion carried a real raise.
It **grew**: −$6,918 selection-free in v3, **−$7,535** in v4, −$8,591 on the
held-out seed.

The mechanism is now visible. Making the promotion expensive pushed the engine
*further* toward the cheap currencies. K15's headline was not conditional on tiny
promotions — it is more true when promotions are real.

## What the objection was right and wrong about

**Right that it mattered.** A 2% promotion with no scarcity was badly
underspecified, and fixing it moved every headline number in the study.

**Right that the PTO-over-promotion ranking was an artifact** — but the artifact
ran the other way. Properly specified, the engine promotes *less*, not more, and
the ranking gets stronger.

**Wrong about the direction of the fix.** Making a promotion a real level change
did not rescue logrolling. It killed the equal-speed claim outright, because the
engine's advantage in v2 and v3 came precisely from paying people in currencies
that cost the employer little — and a real promotion is not one of those.

## What now stands, across four specifications

**Dead:** the equal-speed money claim, in every specification tried.

**Leads everything:** the engine cuts the employee's cash. −$3,395 population,
−$7,535 selection-free, growing as promotions become more real.

**Alive, and the only thing that is:** the clock. **+$29,335 joint** with the
calendar running, departures 35.8% → 14.0%.

**Alive:** the employer takes the overwhelming majority of it.

**New, and uncomfortable:** against a properly specified promotion, a plain
corporate negotiation archetype delivers the employee **more cash and five times
the promotion rate**. If the employee-facing pitch is "our engine gets you a
better deal," that pitch now has a counterexample inside our own study.

## ADDENDUM — where on the frontier? (exploratory, post-hoc)

Asked after the run: *"doesn't that just mean the engine bargains over a
different utility function — aren't we landing on a different point of the
frontier, or playing a different game?"*

**The first half is false and checkable.** `slow_archetype3` scores the crab with
`crab_value3` (arms3.py:168); `crab_issues3` hands `negotiate_bundle` the same
`crab_value3` (arms3.py:73). Identical objective, both arms.

**The second half is exactly right.** `research/molt/frontier.py` enumerates the
package space per crab-season, computes the (crab utility, Works NPV) frontier,
and locates both arms' agreed packages on it. 234 crab-seasons, 3 seeds, both
arms retaining:

| | crab utility | crab cash | Works NPV | Pareto-efficient |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| archetype | 22,024 | 16,714 | −25,159 | **86%** |
| engine | 20,082 | 10,576 | −20,385 | **82%** |

**engine − archetype: utility −$1,942 ± 661, cash −$6,139, Works +$4,774,
joint +$2,832.** The employer takes **169%** of the joint gain the engine finds —
more than all of it, because the employee's share is negative. Both arms sit on
the frontier in 70% of the same crab-seasons. Neither is playing badly.

**So the engine slides along the frontier toward the employer.** It is not
finding the employee a bigger share; it is finding a cheaper way to deliver a
package, and the saving goes to the side paying.

### The correction this forces

RESULTS-V4 above says the engine "wins only on a utility score computed with
exchange rates I chose." That is wrong in an important direction. **Conditional
on the crab staying, the engine loses on that score too** — against all six
reported archetypes:

| slow opponent (both-stay, n≈1,189) | utility | cash |
|---|---|---|
| Split-the-Diff | **−3,041** | −7,535 |
| Anchorer | −3,050 | −7,484 |
| Silent Hardliner | −2,670 | −6,605 |
| Deadline Exploiter | −2,652 | −6,574 |
| Logroller | −3,038 | −7,484 |
| Tactical Empath | −3,038 | −7,456 |

The engine's **population** utility advantage of +$1,006 is therefore **entirely
retention**: departures 35.8% → 14.0%. It scores better on average because more
of its crabs still have a job, not because its packages are better for them.

**The corrected one-line reading:** what the engine offers the employee is that
they still have a job. On the package itself it moves them to a cheaper point on
the frontier and hands the difference to the employer.

## Limitations carried forward, plus two new

Unchanged: the attrition hazard is the load-bearing parameter; ~29–36% annual
departures; the Works knows the crab's priorities; no equilibrium response; no
humans; shadow-price medians unswept.

New to v4:

- **Arms no longer share state.** A consumable quota means arms diverge in *who*
  got a slot, not just in what each crab was offered. Paired per-crab differences
  are still reported; the caveat travels with them.
- **The engine plans one crab at a time.** A quota is a knapsack across crabs, and
  nothing here solves it globally. The version of this product that allocates
  scarce slots across a whole team is untested and is the obvious thing K20 makes
  you want to build.
